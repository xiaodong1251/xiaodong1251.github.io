The Run-of-Show: What a Professional TikTok Live Script Actually Looks Like

Amateur lives are improvised. Professional lives are written. Not word-for-word — nobody reads a teleprompter for two hours — but structured, with beats, timings, and transitions planned before the camera turns on. That document is called a run-of-show, and almost none of the US sellers I've watched use one.

Here's the actual structure professional live hosts run, translated from the Chinese operating standard.

The one-page overview

A working run-of-show fits on one page. If yours is five pages, you'll abandon it mid-live. It answers four questions: what happens, in what order, for how long, and what the fallback is when something dies.

A standard 2-hour TikTok Shop live breaks into eight blocks. Here's the skeleton:

| Time | Block | Job | |---|---|---| | 0:00–0:05 | Opening hook | Stop the scroll, state the deal, pin the hero product | | 0:05–0:20 | Hero product deep demo | Prove the product; first conversion push | | 0:20–0:35 | Product rotation 1 | Two supporting SKUs; social proof readouts | | 0:35–0:45 | Engagement break | Q&A, polls, comment games; reset retention | | 0:45–1:05 | Hero product round 2 | New angle; urgency layer (countdown or stock claim) | | 1:05–1:20 | Product rotation 2 | Fresh SKUs for the second wave of entrants | | 1:20–1:40 | Peak push | Best deal of the session, pinned and repeated | | 1:40–2:00 | Wind-down + close | Recap the offers, thank the room, tease the next live |

Notice the rhythm: sell, breathe, sell harder, breathe. Hosts who sell for 120 straight minutes watch their five-minute retention fall off a cliff around minute 25.

Block-level detail: the opening five minutes

The opening block is the only part worth scripting word-for-word, because it fires before you have any social proof. A structure that consistently holds entry rate:

  1. Second 0–3 (visual hook): the product doing its thing — the pan that doesn't stick, the shirt stretching, the gadget mid-demo. Not your face saying hi.
  2. Second 3–15 (verbal hook): the outcome, not the product. "This is the $23 pan that replaced my $140 one" beats "welcome back to my kitchen."
  3. Second 15–45 (the deal): what's on offer tonight, at what price, compared to what. Price anchoring happens here or nowhere.
  4. Second 45–120 (social proof): read comments, answer one question, pin the product. The room needs to see other humans engaging before it trusts you.
  5. Second 120–300 (first close): demo the hero product properly and ask for the first orders. A room that converts early gets recommended harder.

What the fallback column is for

Every block needs a "if this dies" line in your head. Retention dipped 20% in the last five minutes? Skip ahead to the engagement break. Comment flow stopped? Change rooms — literally pick the product up and move. A live that sticks to the script while the room empties is a script written for an audience that isn't there.

The moderator's copy

The run-of-show isn't just for the host. Your moderator runs off the same document: when to pin which product, which comment to amplify, when to drop the countdown sticker, what the stock-claims actually are. In Chinese live teams this document is shared in the greenroom ten minutes before every session, and the post-live review annotates it — what got cut, what ran long, what the actual timestamps were.

After twenty annotated versions of this document, you don't need a scriptwriter anymore. The document writes itself, because it now reflects your room's real behavior instead of a template's guesses.

Where to start tonight

Don't build a two-hour masterpiece. Take your next live, plan four blocks of fifteen minutes each, and write the opening two minutes word-for-word. That's 80% of the professional standard with 20% of the effort.
